PRD: Future You (PNC 360° Financial Health Navigator)
Nov 7, 2026 · Rajat Singh
TL;DR
Future You is a 360° Financial Health Navigator for PNC. It shows your whole money picture today, then lets you talk out loud to two versions of yourself in 2031, and take the first step of a better plan in one tap.
* The pitch: "Your bank app shows what already happened. Future You shows what happens next, and lets you hear it from yourself."
* The product in one breath: every account in one view → a Financial Health Score built on 8 standard indicators → a forecast of every balance and bill → two futures you can talk to (same path vs. a simple plan) → a what-if simulator inside that conversation → one tap to start the plan.
* The moment judges remember: ask both future selves the same question and hear two very different answers in the same voice. Then change one decision and hear the answer change.
* Why it can win: it covers all 5 of PNC's bullets in one story (section 2). It is built on published research, not guesses (section 3). It is designed around what PNC cares about: trusted, transparent AI and PNC's own products (sections 3 and 11).
* Demo customer: Maya, 27, a nurse in Dallas. Health score 55 ("coping"), about one month of savings cushion, and a credit card at 78% of its limit.
* Prize tracks: PNC's challenge, plus MLH's Best Use of ElevenLabs, which is a separate track (MLH prize page).
* Hard deadlines: register the team on Devpost by Nov 7, 2026 at 11:00 PM (a saved draft doesn't count). Edits close Nov 8, 2026 at 12:00 PM (HackUTD hacker guide).
* Open question: the name. MIT's research project is also called "Future You", so consider submitting as Two Futures.

What the best projects and products teach us
The strongest work in this space does three things: it acts instead of only advising, it feels personal, and it lets people check the numbers. The newest future-self research also says which format changes minds most: two future selves, one for each choice.
Who
	What it is
	Evidence
	Lesson for us
 
	MIT Media Lab "Future You" (2024)
	Text chat with an AI version of yourself at around 60
	Preregistered study, 344 people. Anxiety fell 18% and connection to the future self rose 15%, but agency, optimism and future thinking did not change significantly (poster).
	Talking to a future self works on feelings. Feelings alone don't change behavior, so we attach a real action at the emotional peak.
	MIT and UCLA "future twin" study
	About 200 young adults video-chatted with future selves. One group met two future selves, one for each option.
	34% of the two-futures group reconsidered their choice, against 11% in the control group. People rated realistic visuals least useful (5.16 of 7), below practical logic (5.53) and personal meaning (5.58).
	Our core format is the one that moved people most. Spend effort on solid numbers and personal meaning, not realistic faces. The authors also warn about future selves that push products, and about deepfakes.
	Hershfield's aged-photo studies
	People saw an aged photo of themselves before a savings choice
	Working-age adults were willing to put about 33% more of their pay into retirement.
	Make the future self vivid right at the moment of decision.
	SaverLife Financial Navigator (2025)
	A judgment-free AI guide for lower-income savers, with tips and next steps from member data
	44% of members used it with little promotion. A randomized test showed nearly 50% higher retention after seven weeks.
	Members liked that it doesn't judge, and explaining what data is used built trust. People want different entry points, from quick answers to deeper help. SaverLife avoids giving its AI a human name.
	Bank of America's Erica
	A big bank's assistant for voice and text
	About 3.4 billion interactions since 2018. In 2025, 20.6 million users had nearly 700 million interactions.
	Bank assistants are already normal, and PNC competes with this. We must not be "another Erica" that answers questions about the past.
	Cleo with ElevenLabs voice
	An AI money coach with a strong personality and a voice mode
	Personality lands harder in voice, which builds an emotional connection and makes money nudges more effective.
	Voice plus one steady personality. Skip Cleo-style roasting: it fits Cleo's brand, not a bank's.
	Origin AI Advisor
	Hundreds of checks on your finances, turned into ranked actions and a Health Score
	Origin argues chat alone is reactive, because most people don't know what to ask.
	Lead with the next step instead of waiting for questions. Show one step, not a long list.
	Kommonwealth (hackathon project)
	An AI credit coach that made a to-do list and could mail a dispute letter in one click
	Its builder called real-world action the key differentiator.
	One-click action wins demos. Always ask for a clear yes first.
	DollarPilot (won the LionHacks FinTech track)
	A finance coach, a what-if predictor and a shareable "roast" scorecard
	Won its track with the standard coach-plus-what-if formula and a fun hook.
	Coach plus what-if is now the default, so a fun, shareable hook matters.
	PNC Low Cash Mode
	Alerts, at least 24 hours of extra time, and control over which payments go through when a balance runs low
	PNC's own flagship tool for customer control
	Customer control and transparency are PNC values. Build on that and don't copy it.
	Patterns we build on
1. Act, don't just advise. Kommonwealth, Origin and Cleo all moved from answers to actions.
2. Personal beats generic. Future-self chats and voice personality build feelings that generic chatbots don't.
3. Trust has to be visible. PNC's own competition theme was trusted AI, and SaverLife found that explaining data use built trust.
4. One next step beats a list. People don't know what to ask, so the product should offer the step.
5. Two futures side by side change minds most. In the twin study, 34% reconsidered, against 11% in the control group.
6. Faces matter least, logic and meaning matter most. No face-aging, no voice cloning. Real numbers and a real story.
The gap we fill
Banks answer questions about the past (Erica). Fintechs score you and nudge you (Origin, Cleo). Research tools build the bond with your future self (MIT). None of the products or projects we reviewed combine your real accounts, two future selves you can talk to, and a one-tap action. That combination is our "something no one has thought of yet".
The product: concept, user and the magic moment
Future You turns bank data into a conversation with your own future. You see today, meet two versions of yourself in 2031, try a decision, and start the better path with one tap.
The problem
* Money is scattered. PNC's own challenge says most people keep money in many places and few have one clear view of how they're doing.
* Cushions are thin. In the Fed's latest survey, 63% of adults said they would cover a $400 emergency with cash or its equivalent, so more than a third would not (Federal Reserve).
* Tools explain the past. Bank assistants mostly answer questions about what already happened. Most people don't know what to ask, so a chat box alone doesn't help them (Origin).
* The future feels like someone else's problem. People act more for their future self once that self feels real (section 3).

	How it works
1. See. Every account in one view: checking, savings, CDs, IRAs, auto and personal loans, credit cards and mortgages.
2. Understand. A Financial Health Score from 8 standard indicators, each with a plain reason and the math behind it.
3. Forecast. A 12-month balance forecast with a good, typical and bad range, a 90-day bills calendar, and 5-year paths.
4. Meet. Two future selves in 2031, one on the same path and one on a simple plan. Talk to either one out loud.
5. Act. Try a what-if, then start the plan's first step with one tap.
The magic moment
The judge asks both futures the same question: "Was buying the car worth it?" Same voice, two lives. One says the repairs went on the card and she's still paying it off. The other says she paid it off early and that payment became her savings. Then the judge asks the plan future, "What if I take the Austin job?", and her numbers and her story change live. She ends with: "Want me to set up that $150 transfer?" One "yes" and it's done. The emotional high point leads straight into action.
Design principles
1. Code does the math, AI does the talking. Every number comes from the forecast engine.
2. One next step, not a to-do list.
3. Warm and never judgy, in both futures.
4. Show the math everywhere. Tap any number to see where it came from.
5. Same surprises in both futures. The same $2,000 car repair hits both paths in 2028. Only the cushion is different, so the comparison is fair and not a scare tactic.
6. Built for PNC. PNC's account names (Spend is Virtual Wallet's checking account), a hand-off to a PNC banker, and a clear fit with PNC's coming digital assistant.
7. Voice is optional. Text and live captions are always there.
What it is not
* Not investment advice, and it never pushes products.
* Not a face-aging or voice-cloning app.
* Not a replacement for Low Cash Mode or a human banker. It works next to both.

	Quality bars
* Speed: her first word about 2 seconds or less after a question. The engine recalculates a what-if in under 1 second. Screens load in under 2 seconds.
* Accuracy: no invented numbers in any test. Unit tests for every indicator and for the forecast totals.
* Reliability: the main demo path works without the internet, using saved responses, and resets in one click.
* Accessibility: live captions, readable contrast, and a full text mode.
* Privacy: made-up data only, no real personal data, and API keys kept on the server.
* Look: a phone-shaped PNC-style screen with one font family, generous spacing and big, clear numbers.

	Design rules
* Layout: a phone frame in the center, the activity panel on the right, and nothing else on screen.
* Color: a PNC-style orange accent, navy text and a white background. Gray for the same path, a warm tone for the plan. Red, amber and green only for indicator status, always with an icon so color isn't the only signal.
* Type: one sans-serif family. Key numbers big (32–48 px). Labels in plain words, such as "savings cushion", never "liquidity".
* Motion: numbers count up or down when a what-if lands, the forecast band eases in, and the "Scheduled" badge pops. Nothing moves for decoration only.
* Voice: a big mic button, a waveform while listening, and captions always on.
* Future selves: one simple illustrated avatar used on both sides, never a photo or an aged face. The plan side gets a soft warm glow.
* Branding: use our own product name and icon and mention PNC in words. Don't copy PNC's logo unless organizers say it's fine.

* Customer: name, age, city, pay schedule, take-home pay per paycheck, and the two setup answers.
* Account: one of the 8 types, its name (for example "Spend"), balance, rate, limit, minimum payment, due day and maturity date.
* Transaction: date, amount, merchant, category, account, and whether it repeats.

Health score: how each indicator is computed
Each indicator scores 0 to 100 on a straight line between its two thresholds. A pillar is the average of its two indicators, and the overall score is the average of the four pillars. The bands follow the FinHealth framework: 0–39 vulnerable, 40–79 coping, 80–100 healthy (FinHealth Score toolkit).

Forecast logic
1. Calendar. Paychecks every two weeks. Rent, the car payment, the card payment, subscriptions and yearly bills on their due dates. The CD matures in April 2027.
2. Everyday spending. Each category moves around its 12-month average, by as much as it has moved in the past.
3. Surprises. Each month has a 10% chance of a surprise bill between $300 and $2,000. This is an assumption, and users can see it.
4. 1,000 runs. We simulate the next 12 months 1,000 times. The line is the middle result, and the band runs from the 10th to the 90th percentile.
5. Risk days. The chance that checking drops below $0 in the next 90 days.
6. Five-year paths. Monthly steps with interest: card APR, the loan schedule, savings interest, and IRA growth (5% typical, 2% bad, 8% good, all assumptions). Both paths get the same random surprises and the same 2028 car repair, so only the decisions differ.
7. Output. Yearly snapshots for each path: balances, payoff dates, cushion months, the 8 indicators and the score. All numbers are in today's dollars.
Plan rules (a fixed order, no AI)
1. Cushion under 3 months: an automatic transfer every payday, set to the largest $25 step that keeps bad-case checking above $200. For Maya that's $150.
2. Card over 30% used: windfalls, such as the CD maturing, go half to the card and half to savings.
3. Unused or duplicate subscriptions: cancel them.
4. Card paid off: its payment plus $200 a month goes to the next debt, the car loan.
5. Debts gone: the freed money fills savings to 6 months, then goes to the IRA.
6. Next step: the first action that can be automated today, which is rule 1.
What-if rules
* Raise or pay cut: monthly take-home change = yearly change × (1 − 22% federal − 7.65% payroll tax) ÷ 12. There is no state income tax because Maya lives in Texas. Austin example: +469 a month, minus 170 a month.
* Spending change: added to monthly spending from the start month.
* Savings change: added to the automatic transfer.
* One-time cost: paid from savings first, then the card.
* Job loss: income drops to $0 for the chosen number of months.
* Buy a home: price, down payment and rate give a mortgage payment that replaces rent from the purchase month.
* Wording: every result comes back with one sentence built from a template, such as "Taking the Austin job leaves about $170 more a month after taxes and the higher rent." That gives the agent accurate words to say.

	How we stop made-up numbers

Voice settings
   * Voice: a warm voice from the library, or one designed from a description. Never a clone of a real person.
   * Model: a fast one. If she starts inventing numbers in testing, switch to a stronger one.
   * Loud rooms: use the mic mute control as push-to-talk, and earbuds with a mic.
   * Captions: show every line she says, as she says it.
   * First words: "Hey... it's me. Well, it's you, five years from now." Then one line that differs by path.
Tests
   * By hand: the 10-question table in future-maya-setup.md covers facts, tool use, investment questions, a PIN being shared, "are you real?", and noise.
   * Automatic (P1): a script sends the same questions in text mode and checks that every dollar amount and year in her answers appears in the fact sheet or a tool result. A score such as "10 of 10 grounded" goes on a slide.
When things go wrong
   * A tool fails: she says that part is fuzzy and asks again. The screen shows a retry.
   * The mic fails: switch to typing. It's the same agent.
   * The session drops: it restarts on the same path, and the captions stay on screen.
   * A slow answer: she says "Okay, let me think back..." while the tool runs.

Judge Q&A prep
Fourteen questions PNC's judges are likely to ask, each with a short answer to practice until it comes out in one breath.
   1. How accurate is the forecast? It's a range, not a promise. We simulate the year 1,000 times from her real bills and spending patterns, and the assumptions are one tap away. In production, PNC would check forecasts against what actually happened.
   2. Isn't this just a chatbot? The chat is the front door. Underneath, an engine scores 8 standard indicators, forecasts every account and builds two paths. The agent can change a number only by calling that engine, and you can see every call.
   3. How do you stop the AI making up numbers? Its facts come only from the engine, and new numbers only from tools that return a ready-made sentence. The prompt bans math, and our tests check every answer: 10 of 10 grounded.
   4. Is this financial advice? No. It shows options and trade-offs, never recommends investments, and sends bigger questions to a PNC banker. The one step it suggests is moving your own money into savings, and only after a clear yes.
   5. What data goes to the AI? Only a short fact sheet, never raw transactions or account numbers. The demo uses made-up data. In production it would use consent-based account linking and keep data to a minimum.
   6. How would PNC ship this? As a feature of the digital assistant PNC plans to launch, inside the new app. Low Cash Mode covers the day a balance runs low, and Future You covers the months ahead.
   7. Isn't talking to "future you" a gimmick, or creepy? In the MIT and UCLA study, 34% of people who met two future selves reconsidered their choice, against 11% in the control group. We skip aged faces and voice cloning on purpose, and she says she's an AI.
   8. Why voice? Voice makes the future self feel real and helps people who avoid dashboards. Cleo found that personality lands harder in voice. Typing and captions always work too.
   9. What about people with uneven income? The engine simulates income ups and downs, and the data model supports weekly pay. Andre's persona shows it if we have time to build him.
   10. What's in it for PNC? More savings deposits ($312 million a year in our 1% illustration), more app engagement (SaverLife saw nearly 50% higher retention), and an assistant feature built on trust, which sets PNC apart from question-and-answer bots.
   11. How did you measure financial health? With 8 indicators aligned to the Financial Health Network's framework and its score bands, computed from account data. The formulas are shown in the app.
   12. What did you build, and what did you use? We built the engine, the health scoring, the plan rules, the what-if logic, the screens and the tool layer. ElevenLabs provides speech and runs the agent.
   13. How is this different from MIT's Future You? MIT's is a general life chatbot. Ours runs on real account numbers, shows two paths side by side, and ends with an action. Their research inspired the design.
   14. What if someone is in crisis? She stays kind, stops talking about the plan, and points to 988 and to people they trust. A launched version would add a hand-off to a person.
